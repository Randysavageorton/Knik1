Five types of lab scale bioreactor

Select by sterilisation method and by how many experiments you need to run at once. Each type has its own page with models and options. Sizes are nominal vessel volumes. Working volume is lower, as the questions below explain.

Autoclavable glass bioreactors

A borosilicate vessel with a 316L lid and base, sterilised in your own autoclave before it is filled and connected. It suits smaller volumes, and the clear wall lets you watch the culture, foam and sedimentation directly.

Glass · Autoclave · Mechanical or magnetic drive

View autoclavable glass bioreactors

SIP glass bioreactors

The same glass body, fitted with steam connections so the vessel is sterilised where it stands. It cuts handling and autoclave scheduling for laboratories that run several batches a week.

Glass · Sterilise in place · Mechanical or magnetic drive

View SIP glass bioreactors

Benchtop stainless fermenters

A jacketed 316L benchtop bioreactor designed for in-place sterilisation, up to 50 litres. Stainless steel is commonly chosen for larger working volumes, for processes with pressure requirements, and where the equipment will need fuller documentation.

316L stainless · Sterilise in place · Up to 50 L

View benchtop stainless fermenters

Parallel bioreactor systems

Up to four vessels, in 1, 3, 5, 7 or 10 litre sizes, run from a single control interface in batch, fed-batch or continuous mode. Strains, media and setpoints can be compared under the same conditions.

Glass or stainless · Up to four vessels · One interface

Mini bioreactors

Vessels of 0.1, 0.3, 0.5 and 0.6 litres for early strain screening, where culture material or medium is limited.

Four decisions that shape the specification

Volume is the starting point, but the cultivation process, the sterilisation method and the utilities on site shape the vessel as well. The thresholds below are general selection guidelines, not equipment limits. Vessel material and sterilisation method are separate decisions, so a glass vessel can be built for in-place sterilisation and a stainless vessel can be small.

DecisionCommon starting pointWhen it changes
Glass or stainless steelGlass below about 15 L, where visibility matters and autoclaving is practicalStainless is commonly considered above about 15 L, for specific pressure requirements, or for processes that need fuller documentation
Autoclave or sterilise in placeAutoclave below about 30 LIn place above that size. Every vessel we build above 50 L sterilises in place, and frequent batches can justify it at smaller sizes
Mechanical or magnetic driveMechanical drive with Rushton or pitched-blade impellers, for microbial work that needs high power input and oxygen transferMagnetic coupling for cell culture and shear-sensitive work. It removes the shaft seal, with a lower maximum torque
One vessel or parallelOne vessel for a single processA parallel station, to run up to four experiments under shared conditions

Describing your process before design approval lets us settle these choices early and avoids specification changes later in the project.

Starting configurations by organism

Oxygen demand and shear tolerance differ from one organism to the next, and they influence the configuration more than the vessel type does. The table lists common starting points, not fixed rules. Impeller design, agitation speed and vessel geometry also decide the outcome.

What you growCommon starting pointReason
Bacteria and yeast, such as E. coli, Bacillus and PichiaMechanical drive in glass or stainless, with the impeller chosen for oxygen demandOxygen demand is often high, and many strains tolerate strong agitation
Mammalian or insect cellsMagnetic coupling with a low-shear impeller at moderate speedNo shaft seal means fewer contamination points. Shear depends on the impeller and speed as well as the drive
Plant cells or algaeAirlift or illuminated photobioreactorGentle mixing. Algae also need light throughout the culture
Fungi on solid substrateSolid state fermenterAir and heat move through a packed bed, not a liquid

Moving from bench to pilot scale

Process behaviour changes with scale, and a change of manufacturer adds further variables: vessel proportions, agitation and control logic. Every bench scale bioreactor we build runs on the same control platform and documentation structure as our pilot range, so operators and procedures carry across.

A shared platform makes scale-up more predictable. It does not replace verification. Mixing, oxygen transfer, heat removal and shear still have to be checked at the target volume, and that is the purpose of the pilot run.

A typical seed train runs a 10 litre vessel into a 100 litre secondary seed and then a 1,000 litre production fermenter, with 10 percent inoculation at each stage. Volumes are working volumes.

Questions buyers ask about laboratory bioreactors

More questions

What is the difference between total volume and working volume?

Total volume is the full capacity of the vessel. Working volume is the culture you can run, usually 65 to 80 percent of the total, with 70 percent typical. The remainder is headspace and room for foam. A 10 litre vessel therefore runs at about 7 litres at the typical fill. For any bench scale bioreactor, working volume sets your batch size, and datasheets list both figures.

What is the difference between a laboratory bioreactor and a laboratory fermenter?

The terms overlap. Fermenter is traditionally used for microbial processes such as bacteria, yeast and fungi, and bioreactor also covers cell culture. The specification depends on your organism and process, not on the term.

Can I run several experiments at once?

Yes. A parallel station runs up to four vessels from one control interface, in 1, 3, 5, 7 and 10 litre sizes, in batch, fed-batch and continuous modes.

How do I choose between glass and stainless, autoclave and SIP?

Glass suits vessels below about 15 litres where you want to see the culture. Autoclaving is simpler below about 30 litres. Above those sizes, stainless steel and in-place sterilisation are the usual choice. These are guidelines, not limits. Batch frequency, pressure requirements and your validated process also count.

Can the system be configured for microbial and cell culture work?

Yes. Microbial work normally uses a mechanical drive, and cell culture commonly uses magnetic coupling. Options on any laboratory fermenter we build include ORP, pO2 control, exhaust gas analysis, extra feed lines, weighing and data logging. Tell us the organism and the options at enquiry so the drive, impeller and instruments are matched from the start.

Can I buy one vessel now and scale up later?

Yes. The minimum order is one unit, and many customers return for a pilot system. A lab scale bioreactor from us uses the same control platform as the larger range.

Do you supply GMP documentation at lab scale?

On request. We prepare DQ, IQ and OQ protocols, and your team leads PQ with our support. Qualification documents support validation. They do not by themselves establish GMP compliance, which depends on your facility and process.

What is the lead time?

It depends on the vessel and how much is custom. The quotation gives a date and states what it covers, and we confirm it again at design approval.
